# Self-Editing for Fiction Writers — Browne & King Method

A distilled methodology from Browne & King's craft guide. Rewritten as an actionable editing checklist for use in Style Check and Rewrite passes. ## Core Principle: Trust the Reader

The single posture underlying every technique: readers are intelligent. Explaining what you've already shown, repeating what you've already established, or signposting what the prose already communicates — all of it patronizes the reader and weakens the writing. Every item below is an application of this principle.

---

## Editing Checklist

### 1. Show vs. Tell

- **Default to scenes over summary.** Narrative summary (telling what happened) drains tension. Major plot events and character moments belong in real time, dramatized.
- **Name emotions only when the scene hasn't already shown them.** If the dialogue and action convey that a character is angry, cutting the word "angry" costs nothing and gains trust.
- **R.U.E. — Resist the Urge to Explain.** The most common form: attaching an emotion label to a line of dialogue that already carries it ("she said angrily"). Strip the scaffolding. If the dialogue can't stand alone, fix the dialogue.

### 2. Characterization and Exposition

- **Develop character through behavior, not description.** Telling the reader that a character is stubborn before showing stubbornness does nothing. Show first; let the reader conclude.
- **Delay and drip exposition.** Readers do not need all background information in chapter one. They need it at the moment it becomes relevant. Front-loaded character history and world-building are the most common forms of stalling.
- **Avoid expository dialogue.** If two characters are discussing facts they both already know in order to inform the reader, the dialogue is fake. Find a better delivery mechanism.

### 3. Point of View Discipline

- **Pick a POV and commit within a scene.** Head-hopping (moving from one character's interior to another's mid-scene without a clear break) disorients readers even when they cannot identify the cause.
- **Match the language to the viewpoint character.** The vocabulary, rhythm, and focus of a passage should be filtered through the character whose head the reader is inside. If the narrator would not notice something, do not describe it.
- **Save emotion for the POV character's interiority.** Only the viewpoint character's feelings are accessible without telling.

### 4. Proportion

- **Time spent signals importance.** Whatever gets the most space, readers interpret as most significant. If a minor action gets three paragraphs and a plot-critical decision gets one sentence, the story is out of calibration.
- **Earn the ellipsis.** Readers fill in routine action (walking to a phone, crossing a room) without being shown each step. Cut bridging action that adds no character or tension.
- **Watch pet topics.** If the narrator's hobby, the author's expertise, or a subplot detail gets disproportionate coverage relative to its narrative function, trim it.

### 5. Dialogue Mechanics

- **"Said" is invisible; everything else is not.** Words like *exclaimed*, *intoned*, *breathed*, and *snapped* pull the reader out of the scene. Use them rarely and only when the action is genuinely impossible to signal any other way.
- **Adverbs on dialogue tags signal weak dialogue.** "Said cheerfully" means the line itself did not convey cheerfulness. Fix the line; delete the adverb.
- **Keep attributions simple and consistent.** Name before pronoun (Maria said, not said Maria). Avoid referring to the same character three different ways in one scene.
- **Ellipses for trailing off; dashes for interruption.** Not interchangeable.

### 6. See How It Sounds (Formality / RUE in Dialogue)

- **Read dialogue aloud.** Stilted, formal, or unnatural speech announces itself immediately when heard. If no one would say it, no character should.
- **Use contractions.** "I would not do that" is stilted where "I wouldn't" is not. Characters speak the way people speak.
- **Compress.** Real speech is padded; fictional dialogue is a refined distillation — compressed, purposeful, every line doing double duty (character AND plot or character AND conflict).

### 7. Interior Monologue

- **Interior monologue is powerful — use it surgically.** It grants readers access to unexpressed thought, which film cannot do. Overuse makes prose feel explanatory rather than experiential.
- **Strip thinker attributions when context is clear.** "She thought" and "he realized" are often redundant. If the surrounding prose already establishes whose head the reader is in, the attribution adds nothing.
- **Interior monologue belongs to the POV character only.** A character cannot intuit another character's exact thoughts; they can only observe behavior and infer.
- **Do not use interior monologue to explain what the scene already shows.** If a character's actions demonstrate their resentment, do not then have them think about their resentment.

### 8. Beats

- **A beat is a small physical action that breaks up or punctuates dialogue.** It can substitute for a speaker attribution, establish setting, pace the scene, or reveal character.
- **Beats must be character-specific.** Generic beats (she nodded, he looked out the window, she crossed her arms) are invisible at best, numbing at worst. The best beats reveal something only this character would do.
- **Never repeat a beat within a scene.** If a character has already lit a cigarette, they cannot light another one for dramatic effect fifteen lines later.
- **Match beat rhythm to dialogue rhythm.** A fast, clipped exchange does not need a beat every other line; a long confrontation does.

### 9. Breaking Up Dialogue and Paragraphs

- **White space is not waste — it is pacing.** Dense, unbroken paragraphs signal effortful reading before the reader has processed a word.
- **Paragraph every speaker change in dialogue.** One paragraph per speaker is the baseline. Break further when a character's speech shifts in tone or direction.
- **Use paragraph breaks to create emphasis.** A short sentence on its own line has more weight than the same sentence embedded in a paragraph. Use this deliberately.
- **Blocks of unbroken narrative slow the scene; broken paragraphs accelerate it.** Adjust according to the scene's required pace.

### 10. Once is Usually Enough (Repetition of Effect)

- **Repetition of effect is more damaging than repetition of words.** Establishing the same character trait three times, conveying the same information in two different scenes, or using two metaphors for the same thing — all of these dilute the first instance.
- **Trust that the first time worked.** Novice writers repeat because they are unsure the reader received the point. Cutting the second instance is almost always the right move.
- **Watch for structural repetition.** Two scenes that accomplish the same narrative function (each demonstrating the villain's cruelty, each proving the protagonist's determination) mean one is redundant.

### 11. Sophistication — Common Constructions to Cut

- **Minimize "-ing" simultaneous-action constructions.** "Grabbing her coat, she walked out the door" implies both actions are simultaneous, which is often physically impossible. Use sequential sentences.
- **Minimize "as" constructions.** "As she pulled off her gloves, she turned to face him" buries action in a dependent clause, making it feel incidental. If the action matters, give it its own sentence.
- **Avoid sentimental over-writing.** Prose that insists on its own emotion ("the terrible, soul-crushing weight of grief") performs feeling rather than producing it.
- **Avoid throat-clearing.** Preamble before the story's real start — establishing weather, geography, history — that does not connect to the immediate scene is stalling.

### 12. Voice

- **Voice is not a technique — it is a commitment.** It emerges from a consistent way of seeing and a specific relationship between narrator and world. Mechanics support it; they cannot manufacture it.
- **Voice is earned through specificity.** Vague, general description produces no voice. The specific concrete detail — the one only this narrator would notice — produces voice.
- **Character voice and authorial voice must align.** The prose rhythm and vocabulary should be consistent with the viewpoint character's personality and history.
- **Voice does not require eccentricity.** A clear, unadorned, direct voice is as much a voice as a baroque one. What it cannot be is neutral.
