# Wulf Moon Method: The Super Secrets System for Short Fiction

Wulf Moon (born Michael Dooney) is a Writers of the Future Vol. 35 winner ("Super-Duper Moongirl and the Amazing Moon Dawdler") and the founder of the Wulf Pack Writers community. His Super Secrets system is a numbered body of craft principles published in the WotF forum workshop thread, a DreamForge Magazine column (VERIFY: all dreamforgemagazine.com article URLs return HTTP 403; content reconstructed from excerpts and secondary sources), and the book "How to Write a Howling Good Story." The system is specifically calibrated for short SFF fiction and the Writers of the Future competition.

---

## The 8-Point Plot

Moon's signature structure. It extends a 7-point framework (the lineage traces to Algis Budrys's 7-point structure, NOT Dan Wells's independent 7-point system; the attribution to Budrys is inferred from WotF lineage and unverified) by adding Heart's Desire as the governing emotional core:

1. A **Character**
2. In a **Setting**
3. With a **Heart's Desire**
4. A **Problem** (inciting incident that threatens it)
5. **Try** (the protagonist acts)
6. **Fail** (escalating; each failure raises stakes per Super Secret #22)
7. **Climax** (per Super Secret #34: "A Climax Goes Big Badda Boom")
8. **Validation / Denouement**

Moon's Super-Duper Secret #1: "Take your reader on a DEEP emotional journey." This is the system's north star: structure serves emotion, not the other way around.

---

## Heart's Desire: The Emotional Core

Moon's definition: "a person, thing, or state of being that the protagonist values and desires, something precious they hope to gain, or have and don't want to lose."

Heart's Desire is the engine of emotional investment. Without it, a technically competent plot will not move the reader. Moon cites Lou J. Berger as a case study: Berger sold to a pro market specifically after placing the Heart's Desire on page one where it had previously been absent or buried.

Heart's Desire must appear on the first page. It is the answer to "why does the reader care?" before plot mechanics kick in.

---

## The Magic Sword: Character Growth Engine

Moon calls this his #1 storytelling secret: "a special power the protagonist must attain to win their Heart's Desire by the end of the story." It is an archetype, not a literal object: a skill, an ally, the overcoming of a psychological flaw, a piece of knowledge.

The Magic Sword operates on a three-beat arc:
1. **Plant**: the reader sees the Sword (or its absence) in the opening
2. **Development**: the protagonist cannot yet wield it fully (the character arc is the learning to wield it)
3. **Climactic use**: the protagonist deploys it in the Climax; the moment is surprising yet logically necessary (Moon calls this the Epiphany)

The Magic Sword connects the character arc to the plot arc: the story is the story of the protagonist becoming someone who can use this thing.

## The Opening Formula: SET YOUR STAGE (Super Secret #45)

The opening must establish five elements within the first three paragraphs:
- A **Character** (established with empathy)
- In a **Setting** (genre-appropriate, specific)
- With a **Heart's Desire** (on the page immediately)
- **Genre signals** (what kind of story this is)
- A **Problem** (the inciting pressure)

Super Secret #15 formulation: "a CHARACTER, in a SETTING, with a PROBLEM."

Super Secret #5: "A story is a PROMISE." The opening is a binding contract with the reader on tone, genre, and the emotional experience to come. Moon's ~5-page rule: Act One delivers all five elements within approximately five pages; "by page six your protagonist better be crossing that threshold."

Additional opening Super Secrets:
- **#2**: "DON'T drive to the story" (in medias res; no waking up, eating, commuting)
- **#3**: "Set the hook" (the first question the reader cannot put down)
- **#7**: the title is your first hook
- **#9**: open with your protagonist (not a secondary character or omniscient landscape)
- **#12**: "MAGIC UP FRONT" (reveal the genre element early; do not bury the spec)

## THINGS GET WORSE: The Escalation Engine (Super Secret #22)

Each try/fail cycle must raise the stakes relative to the previous one. Flat escalation (try, fail, try, fail at the same pitch) produces a saggy middle. Moon's escalation rule: every failure must cost the protagonist something meaningful and make the next attempt harder. The pressure compounds through the middle until the Climax is the highest-stakes attempt.

This is the short-story analogue of `${CLAUDE_PLUGIN_ROOT}/references/writing/chapter-hooks.md`'s "yes-but / no-and" engine, compressed into fewer cycles. A short story typically runs one or two full try/fail cycles before the Climax.

## Kill Your Darlings (KYD): The Revision Method

KYD is a named four-phase revision system Moon created in honor of his mentor David Farland. The phases involve: (1) a logline / summary reduction pass; (2) a flow/stream-of-consciousness session to reconnect with the story's emotional center; (3) a "cardio routine" to find the story's beating heart; (4) ruthless cutting of anything that does not serve the Heart's Desire. (VERIFY: the four-phase descriptions above are reconstructed from available excerpts; the full method is in paywalled course content and the book.)

Super Secret #13 runs opposite to KYD: "DON'T OVEREDIT." The system distinguishes between structural revision (what KYD addresses) and recursive line-editing that erodes voice.

---

## Flow State: "Release the Kraken"

Moon argues that the subconscious generates the best story material. The flow state practice is a deliberate protocol for drawing the story up from the unconscious rather than forcing it from the analytical mind. Super Secret #28: "YOU MUST WRITE" (consistency over inspiration; daily practice is what creates access to the flow state).

## The WotF Selling Strategy

Moon's Super Secrets address the Writers of the Future competition specifically:

- **#0**: proper manuscript format (Shunn; name OFF the manuscript; the single most common instant DQ)
- **#11**: name OFF the manuscript (blind judged; approximately 10% of entrants blow this)
- **#1**: "Enter EVERY quarter" (volume is the strategy; do not wait for a "perfect" story)
- **#17 / #24**: "Know thy judge" (Jody Lynn Nye is the Coordinating Judge; read her published work; first reader Kary English reads the first 2 to 4 pages)
- **#37**: aim at a market you can take down (calibrate submission to a reachable target first, climb from there)
- **#38**: cover letter under 200 words, just the facts, top three pro credits, address the editor by name
- **"4 in 36" Bonus Challenge**: four fresh stories per year; volume beats perfection
- **"lone wolves get slaughtered"**: community membership (the Wulf Pack model) produces accountability, wise readers, and market intelligence that isolated writers lack
- **#26**: find a wise reader with more pro sales than you

The word-count sweet spot for WotF is 4,000 to 6,000 words (from the 10,000-word limit), not because shorter is always better, but because Kary English and Moon's own submissions cluster there.

Noble Sacrifice / The Price of Victory: Moon (citing David Farland) argues that the story making the reader cry most is likely to win. A weighty sacrifice at the Climax, proportional to what the protagonist has been fighting for, gives the ending gravity that competent-but-safe endings lack.

## The Character-Reader Bond

Moon's empathy definition: "your pain, in my heart." The Reader/Hero Bond is the condition where the reader adopts the protagonist's goals as their own. This is the mechanism that makes the Heart's Desire and the Magic Sword emotionally operative.

Super Secret #4: "Pick a major emotion and make your reader FEEL it." The emotion precedes the scene; the scene is designed to deliver it, not the other way around.

Character Agency ("I Need a Hero"): passive protagonists are the primary beginner failure mode. The protagonist must act on their own motivation, not be moved around by plot. Moon's three internal arc types: Positive arc (character grows into their potential), Neutral arc ("When Nothing is Everything"), Negative arc ("Descending Into the Dark Side").

---

## POV and Dialogue

Super Secret #25 on POV: for WotF specifically, avoid first-person narration. Moon recommends third-person close (he calls it "3P Intimate POV"), which gives subconscious access similar to close first-person without the retrospective-safety problem that makes first-person killers implausible.

Super Secret #43: "Write Smart Dialogue." Each dialogue beat must advance both plot and character simultaneously. Moon's "Ping Pong Dialogue" drill: strip a dialogue scene to only the spoken lines and read them for forward momentum and distinctness of voice.

Super Secret #39: "Protect your voice." Voice is what makes a story irreplaceable; revision should sharpen the voice, not sand it down.
