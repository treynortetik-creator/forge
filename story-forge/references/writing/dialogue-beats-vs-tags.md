# Dialogue Beats vs. Tags — Rules and Craft

Attribution — telling the reader who spoke — can be handled two ways: a dialogue **tag** (she said) or an action **beat** (She set down the glass.). These are not interchangeable. They serve different purposes, follow different punctuation rules, and create different effects in the reader's mind. Extending **dialogue craft**, which covers basic tag hygiene; this note covers the full decision tree.

---

## Definitions

**Dialogue tag:** A verb clause attached to the quoted speech that names the speaker and — optionally — describes how the speech was delivered. The verb must be one a character performs *with* their voice: said, asked, whispered, shouted, muttered.

**Action beat:** A separate sentence describing what the character does before, during, or after speaking. The action is performed by the body, not the voice: She picked up the mug. He turned to the window. The beat implicitly attributes the adjacent speech without using a tag verb.

The critical rule: a beat is a **separate sentence**. A tag is a **clause attached to the quote**. Mixing them up creates grammatical errors (e.g., treating "she laughed" as a tag — people cannot literally laugh words).

---

## Punctuation Rules

### When the tag follows the quote
```
"I'd like cake," she said.
```
Comma inside the closing quote. Tag is lowercase (unless it starts with a proper noun). Period goes at end of tag.

### When the tag precedes the quote
```
She said, "I'd like cake."
```
Comma after the tag. Quote starts with a capital letter.

### When the tag interrupts the quote
```
"I'd like," she said, "cake."
```
Two commas, both inside the quotes. Second fragment of speech is lowercase because it's a continuation.

### When a beat follows the quote
```
"I'd like cake." She pushed the plate toward him.
```
Period inside the closing quote. Beat starts with capital letter — it's a new sentence.

### When a beat precedes the quote
```
She pushed the plate toward him. "I'd like cake."
```
Each is its own sentence; standard capitalization.

## When to Use Each

| Use a **tag** when... | Use a **beat** when... |
|---|---|
| The conversation is moving fast and you don't want to slow it | The reader should pause and take in something beyond the words |
| Attribution is all that's needed | The action reveals emotion, character, or subtext |
| Two characters are in rapid back-and-forth | The scene needs physical grounding |
| You want to be invisible | You want to show, not tell |

"Said" is the workhorse tag. It is functionally invisible to readers — they process it as punctuation, not prose. Varying tags ("he thundered," "she bristled") pulls readers out of the dialogue to notice the craft. Reserve these for moments that genuinely need emphasis.

Adverbs on tags ("she said angrily") almost always signal that the dialogue itself isn't doing the job. The fix: rewrite the line so the anger is audible in the words, or replace the tag with a beat that shows the anger physically.

---

## The Beat Quality Test

Every action beat should pass at least one of three checks:
1. **Advances story** — the action changes something (a decision made, an object moved, a door opened)
2. **Reveals character** — the choice of action tells us who this person is under pressure
3. **Reinforces theme** — the physical action echoes the scene's deeper meaning (the *Ten Commandments* (1956) example: Rameses drops weights on a scale while accusing Moses; Moses places a heavier brick in response — the conflict is played out physically)

Beats that fail all three are filler. Common filler beats to cut or replace: nodded, smiled, sighed, shrugged, raised an eyebrow, tilted her head. These are the generic gestures that appear in every scene because writers reach for them when they have nothing specific to say. They're not wrong — they're weak.

## The Sanderson Exercise Applied

Sanderson's pure-dialogue exercise (no tags, no beats) teaches what the words must carry on their own. After running the exercise, adding beats back becomes intentional: every beat earns its way because the writer has proven the dialogue can stand without it.
