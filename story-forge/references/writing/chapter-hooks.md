# Chapter Hooks — Ending Each Unit with Forward Pull

A chapter hook is the device that makes a reader turn the page instead of putting the book down. It works by breaking a cause-and-effect sequence at a point of maximum tension — leaving an unanswered question, an unresolved danger, or a disrupted expectation that can only be resolved by continuing. Every chapter (and most scenes) needs one. Cliffhangers are a subset: extreme-intensity hooks placed at chapter or act endings.

This note covers two things: the mechanics of hooks and cliffhangers, and the Try/Fail cycle — the engine that generates the material hooks are built from.

---

## What Makes a Hook

Nick Macari's definition: a hook is "a question the reader MUST have answered." It works by interrupting cause and effect at a psychologically uncomfortable point. The reader has seen the cause; they cannot settle until they see the effect.

Three functional requirements:
1. **Relevance** — the hook must matter to the story. A shocking reveal that doesn't connect to the main throughline produces reader whiplash, not momentum.
2. **Proportional intensity** — not every hook needs cliffhanger-level stakes. Chapter hooks can be quieter: an unanswered question, a character making an unexpected decision, a piece of new information that changes what came before. Cliffhangers reserve maximum intensity for structural turning points.
3. **Earned payoff** — a hook promises a resolution. If the payoff in the next chapter doesn't justify the tension created, readers feel cheated. The hook creates a contract.

---

## Hook Types

### Standard Chapter Hook
Moderate intensity; high relevance to the current story thread. Forms include:

- **Unresolved question**: the chapter ends just before the character learns the answer, or just after they've asked the question that changes everything.
- **Revelation that reframes**: new information at chapter's end makes the reader re-evaluate what came before. The story has changed without the protagonist knowing it yet.
- **Decision point**: the character commits to a course of action whose consequences aren't yet visible. The reader carries the dread forward.
- **Interruption**: the scene breaks at the moment before the anticipated event resolves. This is the cause-without-immediate-effect structure: the reader's brain demands closure.

### Cliffhanger
An extreme-potency hook typically reserved for chapter ends, act ends, or series entries. The definition from Macari: "a break in cause and effect containing an uber-amount of core story elements." Cliffhangers demand immediate continuation and carry their psychological effect beyond the page turn — a good cliffhanger is still working on the reader the next morning.

Warning: cliffhangers lose potency through overuse. If every chapter ends at maximum intensity, intensity becomes the baseline and loses its effect. Alternate standard hooks with cliffhangers, reserving the latter for genuinely structural moments.

## Sanderson's Signposting Framework

Brandon Sanderson (2025 plot lectures) treats chapter structure as a miniature version of the full story arc: every chapter should deliver a **signpost of progress**. Readers tolerate slow stretches when they feel the story is moving; they abandon when they feel it's standing still.

At the chapter level, a signpost can be:
- New critical information that recontextualizes the situation
- An obstacle that closes off one path and opens another
- A relationship shift (someone becomes an ally, or an ally becomes suspect)
- A character decision that commits them to a new course (and reveals character in the making of it)
- An emotional shift — the character's internal state has genuinely changed by chapter's end

The signpost doesn't need to be an event. It needs to be a change. A chapter that ends with the protagonist in the same situation, with the same information, holding the same emotional position, is a chapter that stalls pacing by definition.

## The Try/Fail Cycle — Source of Hook Material

The Try/Fail cycle is the structural engine underneath sustained chapter momentum. The protagonist attempts to solve the central problem. The outcome is never clean success. Instead:

### "Yes, But" (Complicated Success)
The character achieves the immediate goal — but with a complication that creates a new problem. Example: she gets the weapon she needed, but the person who gave it to her now knows she's alive. The try succeeded; the situation is worse.

### "No, And" (Compounding Failure)
The character fails the immediate attempt — and the failure makes things worse. Example: he doesn't get past the guard, and now the guard has raised an alarm. The try failed; the situation is worse.

Both outcomes share the same requirement: **the story cannot return to its prior equilibrium**. Each cycle changes the situation by degrading one of four things:
- **Resources** — time, money, allies, options are spent or removed
- **Information** — the failure reveals something about the problem (the enemy knows more than expected; the approach was wrong)
- **Relationships** — the attempt costs someone's trust or draws unwanted attention
- **Stakes** — the cost of ultimate failure becomes visible and higher than established

Characters typically cycle through two or three try/fail sequences before a crisis forces a new approach or a structural shift. The cycle is what produces the material that chapter hooks cut into — every cycle produces an unresolved question ("will the new approach work?") or a revelation ("the plan has been compromised") that becomes the hook.

### Escalation Is Mandatory
A try/fail cycle where the character simply tries the same thing again at the same stakes is a writing problem, not a technique. Each attempt must either raise the cost of future attempts or reveal that the problem is different from what was understood. Sanderson's observation: each cycle should make the reader believe more strongly that the protagonist cannot succeed — which makes the eventual success feel both surprising and earned.

---

## Practical Execution: The End-Chapter Pass

During revision, run a pass focused only on chapter endings. For each ending, ask:
1. Does this chapter end on a question the reader cannot ignore?
2. Is there an unresolved cause-and-effect beat that demands continuation?
3. If the protagonist is reflecting or resting, is there a piece of information in that reflection that opens a new problem?
4. Does the last line carry a charge — either of dread, curiosity, or anticipation?

If the answer to all four is no, the chapter ends on neutral ground and the reader can comfortably stop. Either push the chapter further into a try/fail cycle, or restructure the break point.
