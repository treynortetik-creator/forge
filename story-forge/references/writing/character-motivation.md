# Character Motivation — Want, Need, Wound, Lie, Weakness

The psychology beneath the arc. **character arcs** covers how Ghost/Lie/Want/Need map onto structural beats. This note goes one level deeper: the precise distinctions between each term, the four layers of desire, how to excavate motivation from scratch, and why flaws are symptoms rather than sources.

---

## The Hierarchy: Ghost → Wound → Lie → Weakness

K.M. Weiland (helpingwritersbecomeauthors.com) defines four related but distinct elements. Confusing them produces fuzzy characterization:

**Ghost** — the backstory *event*. A specific moment in the character's past — traumatic, formative, or simply unfortunate — that planted something wrong in how they see the world. The Ghost is historical and factual within the story's logic. It may be on the page or held off it for mystery; either way, its existence must be *felt*.

**Wound** — the *emotional residue* the Ghost left behind. Not the event itself, but the ongoing psychological pain: the generalized suffering, the rawness that hasn't healed. The Wound is what the character is still carrying. It predisposes them toward the Lie.

**Lie** — the false *belief* the character built to explain or protect against the Wound. It is always a worldview error: a misread of themselves, other people, or how the world works. Weiland's key distinction: the Lie is not the Wound and is not the Weakness. It is the cognitive distortion sitting between them. Example: Scrooge's Lie is "a person's worth equals their material wealth" — not his abandonment (Ghost), not his loneliness (Wound), and not his cruelty (Weakness).

**Weakness** — the *behavioral manifestation* of the Lie. What others observe: a moral failing, a destructive habit, a social dysfunction. The same Lie can produce different Weaknesses in different characters. The Weakness is what gets the character into trouble plot-wise; the Lie is what makes the Weakness make sense.

The chain: Ghost **causes** the Wound → Wound generates the Lie → Lie expresses as Weakness.

A flaw that isn't traceable to a Lie is generic decoration. A Weakness that grows from the Lie is structural — it will resurface under pressure and drive the arc.

## The Four Layers of Desire

From Weiland's "What Does Your Character Want?" — four distinct things characters want, often conflated:

1. **Desire** — the deep, subconscious longing that predates the story. Universal, emotional, not necessarily plot-specific. Examples: to be loved, to matter, to feel safe. Usually unstated by the character; often the emotional throughline of an entire series.

2. **Plot Goal** — the specific external objective driving this story. What the character is actively pursuing in chapters 1 through the climax. Concrete and measurable.

3. **Moral Intention** — the character's internal rule about *how* to pursue the goal. What they believe is the right or acceptable method. Often where moral complexity lives: a character might have a good Plot Goal but a corrupted Moral Intention.

4. **Need** — the Truth the character must internalize to complete their arc. Not an object or outcome; a realization. Almost always in direct tension with the Plot Goal (the Want). Accepting the Need usually requires releasing or sacrificing the Want.

The inner motor of Act 2 is the tension between the Plot Goal (Want) and the Need. They cannot coexist indefinitely.

## Motivation Excavation (Working Backward)

The practical process for discovering a character's motivation structure when starting from behavior rather than backstory:

1. **Observe the behavior**: What does the character do compulsively? What do they avoid? What do they lie about, even to themselves?

2. **Surface the Lie**: What belief system would make that behavior *logical* or *self-protective*? A character who never asks for help believes something specific about what needing help means.

3. **Find the Wound**: What ongoing pain does the Lie protect? The Wound is usually an emotion the character cannot let themselves feel — grief, shame, fear of abandonment.

4. **Identify the Ghost**: What specific event created that Wound? Work backward until you find the inciting moment.

5. **Derive the Weakness**: Given this Lie, what behavioral patterns will the character repeat even when those patterns hurt them or others?

This order (behavior first, Ghost last) works better for character-led stories. For plot-led stories, running it forward (premise → Ghost → Wound → Lie → behavioral consequences) integrates more easily with `${CLAUDE_PLUGIN_ROOT}/references/writing/outlining-method.md`.

## The 3 M's: Per-Scene Motivation Filter

From Weiland's "7 Tips" article — a quick filter for any scene:

- **Mindset**: What is this character's current state of mind as the scene opens? Attitude, preoccupation, emotional weather.
- **Motivation**: Why are they taking the action this scene demands? Is the root motivation visible — even if only to the reader?
- **Mood**: What emotional register are they operating in? How does it color what they notice, how they speak, what they misread?

If a character's action in a scene cannot be traced back through these three to the Ghost/Lie/Wound, the action is probably plot-convenient rather than character-driven.

## Inner Motivation vs. Goal-Driven Motivation

Two types of motivation operate simultaneously and must connect:

**Inner motivation (core need)**: Subconscious, tied to the Wound. Drives the character to seek the story's thematic resolution whether they know it or not. Example: a character may consciously want to win a competition but unconsciously be seeking proof they are worthy of love.

**Goal-driven motivation**: Tied to the Plot Goal and visible in the story's external engine. What the character tells themselves and others they're doing.

When these two are well-integrated — when the Plot Goal *also* scratches the Inner itch — the story feels inevitable. When they're disconnected, the character feels plot-instrumental.

---

## Structural Flaws vs. Decorative Flaws

A structural flaw grows directly from the Lie. Remove the Lie and the flaw stops making sense. It pressures the protagonist, it costs them something real, and it must be overcome (or consciously chosen) at the climax.

A decorative flaw (a quirk, an eccentricity, a harmless vice) adds texture but carries no narrative weight. It will not generate conflict or block the protagonist from the Need.

Both have a place. But a character's *defining* flaw should be structural — traceable all the way back to the Ghost.

## Checklist

Before locking a major character:
1. State the Ghost (one specific event).
2. State the Wound (the emotional pain it left).
3. State the Lie (the false belief built to survive the Wound).
4. State the Weakness (the behavior the Lie produces).
5. Verify: does the Plot Goal connect to the Lie? (The character pursues the Want because the Lie tells them it will fix the Wound.)
6. Verify: does the Need contradict the Lie? (Accepting the Need requires dismantling the Lie.)

If 5 and 6 hold, the motivation structure is load-bearing.
