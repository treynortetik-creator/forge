# Information Control — Foreshadowing, Reveals, and Dramatic Irony

Information control is the discipline of governing exactly what the reader knows at each moment of a story. It encompasses planting, withholding, misdirecting, and timing. Every reader-writer transaction is an information transaction: the writer deposits and withdraws data, and the resulting gap between reader knowledge and character knowledge determines whether the reader feels suspense, curiosity, dread, or shock. Getting that gap wrong in either direction kills the story. Too little withheld and the ending is obvious; too much and the reader disengages. The craft is calibration.

---

## Planting and Payoff: Chekhov's Gun

The foundational principle: every significant element introduced must pay off; every payoff must have been planted. Chekhov stated it in letters (1889): if a gun appears on the wall in Act One, it must fire by Act Three. The reverse is equally binding: if the gun fires, it must have appeared earlier.

A plant works when the reader does not recognize it as a plant. The best plants are smuggled in as texture: a character detail, a throwaway line of dialogue, a piece of setting. The reader absorbs them without flagging them as information. When the payoff arrives, recognition is instant and retroactive.

**Three levels of plant quality:**
- **Invisible plant**: hidden in plain sight, fully justified by context; reader re-reads and thinks "it was there the whole time"
- **Visible plant**: obvious setup that telegraphs the payoff, producing anticipation rather than surprise (useful when dramatic irony is the goal)
- **Retroactive plant (cheat)**: introduced after the payoff to paper over a gap; readers who re-read notice and feel cheated

The plant-payoff interval matters. A gun planted one scene before it fires is mechanical. A gun planted two acts before it fires is satisfying. The longer the interval, the deeper the embedding needs to be in scene texture, and the stronger the payoff lands.

## Types of Foreshadowing

**Direct foreshadowing**: an explicit signal that something is coming. ("She didn't know then that she would never see him again.") This creates anticipation and dread but sacrifices surprise; use it when the emotional weight of waiting matters more than the shock of arrival.

**Symbolic or indirect foreshadowing**: an image, a pattern, or a motif that carries associative weight without explicit statement. A character repeatedly avoiding mirrors before a scene that exposes a truth about her. Weather. Color. Recurring objects. Brandon Sanderson calls this "seeding" and distinguishes it from direct foreshadowing: seeds are placed so the author can grow them later, without announcing the fact. Seeds that pay off feel earned; seeds that don't pay off feel like loose threads.

**Atmospheric foreshadowing**: the emotional register of a scene signals what is coming even when no specific content does. Unease in the prose before violence; a too-peaceful scene before disaster. This works because readers sense tonal dissonance even if they cannot name it.

**False foreshadowing (red herring)**: a planted element that appears to signal one outcome and resolves differently, or not at all. The technique creates the illusion that the narrative world is rich with possibility while guiding attention away from what is actually coming. Agatha Christie's entire method depends on disciplined red herrings that remain fair: plausible, planted cleanly, and achieved through character misdirection rather than narrator suppression.

## The Three Knowledge-States

The relationship between reader knowledge and character knowledge generates three modes of tension, each with a distinct emotional signature:

| Configuration | Name | Reader Experience | Primary Emotion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reader knows MORE than character | Dramatic irony | Watches character walk toward danger she cannot see | Dread, suspense |
| Reader knows LESS than character | Mystery / curiosity | Senses withholding; must read forward to understand | Curiosity, unease |
| Reader and character discover together | Suspense / race | Information arrives in real time; stakes are immediate | Tension, identification |

Dramatic irony is the strongest sustained tool for dread. Once the reader knows the monster is in the house, every scene set there carries freight the characters do not feel. Mystery withholds enough to compel forward reading but must give enough orientation to prevent frustration; readers who cannot form any hypothesis disengage. The reader-and-character-together mode is the default register of most fiction, producing identification and momentum.

Most page-turners cycle through all three. Extended dramatic irony earns the right to be broken by genuine surprise (reader and character simultaneously shocked). Mystery resolves in stages, which produces the ladder structure covered below.

## The Reveal: Surprising Yet Inevitable

John Truby identifies the reveal as the mechanism by which the story's deeper logic becomes visible to the reader. A well-earned reveal is both surprising and inevitable: surprising because it was hidden; inevitable because every piece of evidence was present, correctly planted, and sufficient. The reader's reaction is not "I never could have guessed" but "how did I miss it."

The seeds of a reveal must be planted before the reveal, not after. The critical technique is honest misdirection: plant the truth in a context where the reader interprets it as something else. Christie's narrators present clues in a stream of detail where the significant and the insignificant are indistinguishable, a method so reliable it became a formal model.

Ronald Knox's "Detective Story Decalogue" (1929) codified what fair-play revelation requires: the guilty party must be introduced early; all physical clues must be available to the reader; no accident may solve the case; no unknown twin or double may appear. These are not genre quirks but a contract: the reader agreed to play the game, and the writer agreed to give them a real chance to win. Violating these rules does not create surprise; it creates betrayal.

---

## The Reveal Ladder

A story with one reveal has one gear. The stronger structure is the reveal ladder: a sequence of disclosures, ordered by significance, each one raising the stakes for the next. Story Grid treats revelation as a structural commandment rather than an ornament: the progressive disclosure of story truth is what produces the experience of the narrative "opening up" across acts.

**The ladder has three properties:**
1. Each rung must make the previous rungs feel more significant, not less. A reveal that trivializes earlier reveals breaks escalation.
2. Each rung must create a new question or make an existing question more urgent. A reveal that closes all open loops is an ending; intermediate reveals should open at least as much as they close.
3. The final rung must retroactively justify every earlier rung. The climactic disclosure is the one that makes the whole architecture visible. Sanderson calls this "the payoff that earns the promise."

Information is not just revealed; it is rationed. Giving the reader everything at once produces a document, not a story. The ladder forces the writer to sequence discoveries according to emotional and logical impact, and to withhold the highest-value disclosure until the narrative has fully earned it.

## Withholding Without Cheating

Withholding is legitimate when the narrator withholds what the narrator does not know. Withholding is a cheat when the narrator suppresses information that their POV clearly makes available, for no reason other than the writer's convenience.

The test: if a reader can reasonably ask "why didn't she think about X right now?" and the only honest answer is "because the author needed you not to know yet," the withholding is a cheat. The fix is either to establish a psychological reason for the character to avoid that thought (dissociation, denial, trained evasion) or to restructure so the reader does not occupy that POV at that moment.

**Craft options for honest withholding:**
- Give the POV to a character who genuinely lacks the information
- Allow the character to perceive without understanding (sensory detail without interpretation)
- Use a time skip or POV switch to pass over the moment of knowing
- Establish in advance that this character does not think analytically under stress

The Christie standard: every clue must be present for the reader. Withholding is achieved through camouflage (presenting the clue without signaling its significance), not suppression (concealing that the clue exists at all). The narrator can lie by emphasis; the narrator cannot lie by omission.

## Misdirection Technique

Misdirection redirects the reader's attention from the significant to the plausible-but-wrong. The mechanism is attention: readers track what the narrative rewards their attention for tracking. Load a scene with a suspicious character who speaks in evasions, and the reader will attend to the evasions. The actual clue can sit in the same scene, unattended.

**Three primary misdirection tools:**
- **The false suspect**: a character who has means, motive, and opportunity, presented so as to absorb suspicion that belongs elsewhere
- **The misleading detail**: a planted element that is real but whose significance is misread; the reader is given the right answer and the wrong interpretation
- **The misleading structure**: genre conventions manipulate expectation; the reader anticipates the detective will solve the case, so a narrative where the detective is systematically wrong operates as structural misdirection

Misdirection requires the writer to know where the truth is, because the misdirection must be accurate about everything except one thing. Misdirection that is simply factually wrong about the story's own events is a cheat, not a craft tool.

## Multi-POV Information Management

When a story runs more than one POV character, information control becomes structural. The writer must track, per chapter: what this character knows; what the reader knows entering the chapter; what the reader will know leaving it; and what the reader must not yet learn.

Truby recommends building a knowledge grid for multi-POV work: a matrix of characters against information items, tracking who knows what and when. Without this map, the writer will accidentally let information leak through the wrong POV (a character thinks something they could not know because the writer knows it from another scene), or will fail to build the dramatic irony that a dual-POV structure enables (the reader sees both sides; each character sees only one).

**The multi-POV dramatic irony opportunity**: once the reader knows from POV-A that the antagonist is in the building, every POV-B scene set there carries dread the POV-B character does not feel. This is a precision instrument. Structure the POV sequence so dramatic irony is active during the scenes where dread is the intended effect, and deactivate it before the scenes where genuine surprise is the goal.

## Promise / Progress / Payoff

Sanderson's framework applies directly to information control. Every story makes a promise about what kind of question it will answer. Progress means delivering partial answers and narrower-focused questions to maintain engagement between the opening promise and the final payoff. Payoff means honoring the promise fully, in a form that exceeds what the reader could have predicted.

Applied to information control: the "promise" is the mystery question raised at the story's outset; "progress" is the reveal ladder, each rung resolving one question while opening another; "payoff" is the climactic reveal that answers the original promise. Mid-story reveals that accidentally answer the central mystery are structural failures. They deliver the payoff before the progress has been fully run, leaving the story no place to go.

The practical check: after every major reveal, ask whether the story's central question is now answerable. If yes, that reveal is the climax; do not use it in Act Two.

## Story Seeding as Proactive Planting (Fox)

Fox's "story seeding" is a specific plant category not covered above: embedding references to other books, poems, plays, or invented works inside the story, deployed for the same underlying purpose as any plant (reader payoff) but via intertextual reference rather than a physical object or plot fact.

- **The touch-point technique**: dense name-dropping of beloved works for a cumulative pleasure-jolt and genre/mood signaling (*Ready Player One*). Risk: reads as manipulative if the references don't organically serve the story; dial back if it starts to feel like a name-drop quota.
- **Signal character intelligence**: a character's reference to a work or myth counterbalances other characterization signals that might read as "dumb" (*Salvage the Bones*, the Medea myth mapped onto the protagonist's own crisis).
- **Thematic reinforcement**: reference a work running thematically parallel to the story, giving readers an immediate emotional touchstone (*Sunrise on the Reaping* referencing Poe's "The Raven" for shared grief and a shared name).
- **Sprinkle quotes**: use quotes from philosophy or fiction as a thematic backbone (Sally Rooney quoting Marx in *Beautiful World, Where Are You?*); a character misquoting something, especially if corrected by another character, is a compact characterization tool.
- **Fake books work too**: invent fictional books and authors inside the story world (Bolaño, Borges). Especially useful for fantasy or sci-fi not set in our universe, where real-world references would break immersion.
- **Incorporate into the action**: make the referenced work structurally load-bearing to the plot itself, not just texture. *Station Eleven*'s survivors performing Shakespeare after a plague function as plot and theme engine, not decoration.
- **Borrow the culture**: import an entire cultural tradition, not just one book, and have characters reenact it in a modern register (*The Secret History* borrowing Greek classical ritual and party culture).
- **Offer hilarity**: use the reference for comic payoff: inside jokes, parody, or a character literally trying to live by a book's rules (Joe Hill's *The Fireman* nodding to his father Stephen King's *The Stand*).

This is a distinct plant type from Chekhov's Gun above: the payoff is resonance and texture, not a plot mechanism firing. Same governing rule applies: organic to story logic beats impressing the reader for its own sake.

## Applied

*G. Rench* runs an explicit two-layer information control system: firewalls (facts the reader must not learn until their designated chapter) and a reveal ladder (the ordered sequence of disclosures across the seventeen chapters). These are a worked instance of the principles above. Firewalls are the "honest withholding" mechanism: they require the dual-POV structure to assign each reveal to the correct character-chapter, so neither Grant nor Cindy accidentally surfaces a piece of information ahead of its rung. The reveal ladder is the "progress" phase between the opening promise (who is Grant, what is he doing, will Cindy stop him) and the final payoff. Because the story runs two POVs with asymmetric knowledge (Grant has full access to his own psychology; Cindy is closing an information gap), the dramatic irony mode is available in every Cindy chapter once Grant's intention is established. The firewall system is what keeps that dramatic irony productive rather than collapsing into premature resolution.
