# Hook rubric

Seven axes, each scored 1-5. **A strong commercial pitch usually sits at 4-5 on most of these; 3 is
"serviceable but generic."** Score every candidate before picking one, and write the scores down — an
unscored comparison is a preference, not an evaluation.

```
High-level criteria (for pitches)
Score each from 1–5:

Immediate curiosity – Does the pitch raise a clear “why/what next?” question as soon as you hear it?

Clear story promise – Can you tell what kind of story this is (genre, tone, scale, target reader) from the pitch alone?

Concrete stakes or tension – Is there a specific problem, risk, or dilemma baked into the pitch, not just a situation?

Distinctive protagonist – Does the pitch spotlight a particular character with a notable role, trait, or predicament?

Concept clarity – Is the core idea understandable in one read, without needing extra explanation?

Marketable angle – Does the pitch offer a fresh twist or angle on a recognizable commercial premise?

Forward drive – Is there a clear sense of trajectory (what the character must do, confront, or decide next)?

A strong commercial pitch will usually sit at 4–5 on most of these; 3 is “serviceable but generic,” and 1–2 means the pitch likely won’t stick in a busy agent’s or reader’s mind.

Detailed pitch rubric
You can use these as checklist questions while you refine a logline or short pitch.

1. Curiosity and hook
Does the pitch contain an inherent “something’s off / something big is about to happen” element?

After hearing it once, would a stranger plausibly ask, “Okay, so what happens?”

2. Story promise (genre and tone)
Can someone guess the genre and rough tone (eg high-stakes thriller, cozy mystery, dark romantic fantasy) from the pitch alone?

Does the pitch imply the emotional experience (fear, swoon, catharsis, laughter, etc.) the story will deliver?

3. Stakes and tension
Is there a clearly stated or strongly implied or else (what goes wrong if the character fails)?

Are the stakes concrete (a relationship, a job, a life, a kingdom, a secret) rather than airy or abstract?

4. Protagonist magnetism
Does the pitch identify a specific protagonist (or tight ensemble) rather than “people” in general?

Is there at least one distinctive attribute or situation that makes this protagonist memorable (eg “disgraced surgeon,” “reluctant cult heir,” “widowed wedding planner”)?

5. Concept clarity
Can you say the pitch out loud in one or two sentences without tripping over it or needing to add “what I mean is…”?

Could someone repeat the essence of it to a friend after hearing it once?

6. Marketable angle and originality
Does the pitch combine familiar elements in a way that feels fresh, ironic, or high-concept (“Die Hard in a retirement home,” “The Bachelor but the suitors are ghosts”)?

If you strip it down to “X must do Y before Z,” does it still sound distinctive compared to others in the genre?

7. Forward drive
Does the pitch make it clear what the protagonist is trying to do (goal), not just what their situation is?

Is there a sense of direction or escalation (eg “must solve,” “has to choose,” “races against time,” “is forced to join”)?

Simple scoring table for pitches
You can apply this table when you’re evaluating or iterating:

| Category         | 1–2 (weak)                             | 3 (okay)                              | 4–5 (strong)                                                             |
| ---------------- | -------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Curiosity        | Feels flat, no implied question.       | Mildly interesting but familiar.      | Immediately suggests an intriguing “why/what next?”.                     |
| Story promise    | Genre/tone unclear or muddled.         | Genre mostly guessable.               | Genre and emotional ride are obvious.                                    |
| Stakes/tension   | No clear problem or risk.              | Vague or low-stakes problem.          | Concrete, meaningful stakes are central to the pitch.                    |
| Protagonist      | Generic “someone/people,” no identity. | Named but lightly characterized.      | Distinctive protagonist with a memorable role/trait/situation.           |
| Concept clarity  | Confusing, overstuffed, or too vague.  | Understandable but clunky or wordy.   | Clean, easy to repeat, instantly graspable.                              |
| Marketable angle | Feels derivative, nothing stands out.  | Familiar but competent.               | Fresh angle or high-concept hook within a recognizable commercial space. |
| Forward drive    | Describes a setup, not what happens.   | Hints at direction but lacks urgency. | Clear goal/obstacle; you can feel the story wanting to move.             |
```
