# Save the Cat — Blake Snyder's 15-Beat Sheet (BS2)

Blake Snyder's 2005 book gave Hollywood screenwriters — and eventually novelists — a named, timed framework of 15 beats that subdivides the three-act structure into precise, functional moments. The innovation over raw three-act thinking is **surgical specificity**: not just "Act 2 begins here" but *what kind of scene* must appear and *why* it must appear at that percentage of the story.

Snyder called his system the "BS2" (Blake Snyder Beat Sheet, with obvious self-deprecating humor). It maps cleanly onto three-act proportions: Act 1 is beats 1–6, Act 2 is beats 7–13, Act 3 is beats 14–15. He described acts as **thesis** (the world as it is), **antithesis** (the world turned upside down), and **synthesis** (the transformed world).

---

## The 15 Beats

Percentage targets are for a 110-page screenplay. Novelists adapt proportionally.

### Beat 1 — Opening Image (~1%)

A snapshot of the protagonist's world *before* the story changes it. Tone, world, and character flaw are all encoded here. Its job is to be the visual/thematic opposite of the **Final Image** (beat 15) — together they bracket the transformation arc. If you can't describe the contrast between your Opening and Final images in one sentence, the transformation isn't clear enough.

### Beat 2 — Theme Stated (~5%)

A character — rarely the protagonist — states the theme of the film aloud, usually as a question or provocation. The protagonist doesn't understand it yet, doesn't believe it, or dismisses it. The entire story is the protagonist learning why the theme is true. Snyder was emphatic: the theme is *stated*, not implied. Often it sounds like unsolicited advice.

### Beat 3 — Setup (1–10%)

Extended introduction of the protagonist's world, relationships, and the specific *flaws and needs* they will overcome. Snyder's rule: set up everything that pays off. Every character, every problem, every comic bit introduced here should transform or resolve by the finale. The setup must also plant the **Six Things That Need Fixing** — the protagonist's life problems that the story will address.

### Beat 4 — Catalyst (~10%)

The inciting incident. Something happens *to* the protagonist from outside their world. This is the destabilizing event that makes the old world untenable. In Snyder's formulation, the catalyst is usually a phone call, letter, new arrival, or external event — something that drops into the protagonist's life and demands response.

### Beat 5 — Debate (10–20%)

The protagonist resists the journey. They ask: *should I do this? Can I? What if I don't?* This is the last moment before commitment. The debate reveals character through the specific shape of their resistance — what they're afraid to lose by going on the adventure. The debate ends when the protagonist makes an active choice (or is forced) to commit.

### Beat 6 — Break Into Two (~23%)

The protagonist crosses the threshold into Act 2. This is a **proactive choice** — the protagonist must choose to enter the new world, not be pushed there. The world of Act 2 is the antithesis of Act 1. The story problem shifts from "will they engage?" to "how will they survive?"

### Beat 7 — B Story (~27%)

A new character arrives, or a dormant relationship activates, carrying the **theme**. This subplot is the emotional and thematic heart of the story — where the internal change happens. The A Story carries the external plot; the B Story is where the protagonist's soul is at stake. In romantic comedies this is often the love interest; in other genres it may be a mentor, a rival who becomes an ally, or a foil. The B Story typically resolves just before Break Into Three.

### Beat 8 — Fun and Games (~27–50%)

Snyder's most quoted beat. This is "the promise of the premise" delivered — the reason the audience bought the ticket or opened the book. If your premise is "a hit man becomes a kindergarten teacher," this is where you show that comic premise explored fully. The fun and games section is **lighter in tone** than what precedes and follows it. It is the A Story running at full speed in the new world. Internal change is not yet happening — the protagonist is still operating on old rules, even if they're succeeding.

### Beat 9 — Midpoint (~50%)

A pivot beat. Either a **false victory** (protagonist appears to win, stakes secretly rising) or a **false defeat** (protagonist faces a reversal that paradoxically makes them more determined). The midpoint also raises the stakes: the story's personal/internal stakes become public/external — "the fun is over." The protagonist shifts from reactive to proactive after the midpoint. The antagonist's forces, which have been passive, now actively pursue.

### Beat 10 — Bad Guys Close In (50–75%)

The A Story's antagonist — whether a person, organization, or internal demon — tightens the vise. External bad guys regroup and advance. Simultaneously, internal bad guys (doubt, ego, old wounds) resurface within the protagonist's own circle. Alliances fracture. The team the protagonist assembled during Fun and Games begins to fail them. Each complication escalates from the last.

### Beat 11 — All Is Lost (~75%)

Rock bottom. The protagonist suffers the single worst moment of the story — a loss larger than anything that came before. Snyder identifies what he calls the **"whiff of death"**: something dies here, literally or metaphorically. A relationship ends, a dream collapses, a mentor dies, an identity is destroyed. The old world's tools have definitively failed.

### Beat 12 — Dark Night of the Soul (75–80%)

The protagonist sits in the ruins of All Is Lost and reckons with it. This beat is pure interiority — they must find the answer *themselves*, from within, before they can act again. External helpers and advice are absent. This is where the theme (planted at beat 2) is finally understood. Without this beat, the protagonist's transformation in Act 3 feels unearned — they changed because the plot required it, not because they actually grasped something new.

### Beat 13 — Break Into Three (~80%)

The protagonist integrates what they've learned and makes a **synthesis decision** — they find the solution that combines the best of Act 1's world and Act 2's new world. This is the eureka moment. Act 3 follows as a direct result of this decision. The B Story resolves here or just before, providing the emotional fuel for the protagonist's final push.

### Beat 14 — Finale (80–99%)

The protagonist executes the synthesis. In Snyder's formulation, the finale has its own five-part structure: gathering the team, executing the plan, hitting a high tower surprise (an unexpected final obstacle), digging deep down (the protagonist finds a reserve they didn't know they had), and executing the climax. Old characters and themes from the setup reappear and are resolved.

### Beat 15 — Final Image (~99–100%)

The mirror of the Opening Image. Shows the transformed world — not described, but shown in a concrete visual/sensory moment. If the Opening Image showed isolation, the Final Image shows connection. If the Opening Image showed cowardice, the Final Image shows courage. The two images together *prove* that transformation occurred.

## Core Craft Principles

**Specificity as discipline.** Snyder's rule: "If I can't fill in the blank in one or two sentences — I don't have a beat yet." The beat sheet forces writers to identify *exactly what happens* at each structural moment before writing prose.

**The transformation axis.** Every beat either (a) expresses the protagonist's original flaw, or (b) moves them toward overcoming it. Beats that do neither are probably dead weight.

**Genre categories.** Snyder classified stories into 10 genres based on the *nature of the transformation* (not setting): Monster in the House, Golden Fleece, Out of the Bottle, Dude with a Problem, Rites of Passage, Buddy Love, Whydunit, Fool Triumphant, Institutionalized, and Superhero. Genre determines what obligatory beats must include — a Rites of Passage story must have a "life wound" at All Is Lost; a Buddy Love story's "All Is Lost" must break the relationship.

**Criticism to absorb.** The beat sheet has been criticized for producing mechanical, predictable stories when followed rigidly. The defence: the beats describe *what must happen emotionally*, not *how it must happen on the page*. The Dark Night of the Soul beat exists in virtually every story that works; the question is whether the writer has *earned* it or is just hitting it on cue.
